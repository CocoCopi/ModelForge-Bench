A city wakes slowly. The first buses move along empty streets, their lights
reflecting on wet pavement left by the night rain. Shopkeepers raise shutters
and arrange fruit in neat rows, and the smell of bread drifts from a corner
bakery. Office workers appear in ones and twos, holding cups and checking
phones, and the crossings fill with the steady rhythm of people who know
exactly where they are going.

By midmorning the market is loud. Vendors call out prices, bicycles weave
between carts, and a musician plays an old song near the fountain. Tourists
stop to listen while residents walk past as if the melody were part of the
weather. In the park, a gardener waters the flower beds and a group of
retired men argue cheerfully over a board game on a stone table.

The afternoon brings heat and a long pause. Cafes put out extra chairs under
the awnings, and students read with their notebooks open beside glasses of
iced tea. A delivery rider waits at a red light, checks an address, and is
gone before the signal changes again. Somewhere a dog sleeps in the shade of
a parked car, unbothered by the traffic.

Evening changes the city again. Windows glow, restaurants set tables on the
sidewalk, and the smell of grilled food mixes with the cooling air. Friends
meet at the station and decide, after some debate, where to eat. A child
counts the lights on a bridge and announces the total to nobody in
particular. The day ends the way it began, with small ordinary things that
are easy to overlook and hard to replace.
