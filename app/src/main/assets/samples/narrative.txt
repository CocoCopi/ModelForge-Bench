The lighthouse had been dark for three winters when Mara finally climbed the
hill to see it. The path was overgrown but still visible, a pale line through
the grass that ended at a white door swollen with salt. She pushed it open
and stood in the round room at the bottom, listening to the wind move through
the tower above her like a long breath.

Her grandfather had kept the light for forty years. He used to say that a
lighthouse does not stop the sea; it simply gives a ship one honest fact to
navigate by. Mara had thought that was a clever thing to say when she was
young. Now she thought it might have been the only thing he truly believed.

She found the lamp in its case under the stairs, wrapped in oilcloth. The
glass was cracked along one edge but the brass was clean, as if someone had
polished it recently and then thought better of using it. She carried it up
the spiral steps, past windows that showed the grey sea on every side, and
set it on the iron table at the top.

That night she lit it. The beam turned slowly, patiently, and the dark water
answered with small flashes of its own. A fishing boat far out changed course
by a few degrees. Mara did not see it happen, and the boat never knew she was
there, but in the morning the harbor keeper left a note under the door with
two words on it: thank you.
